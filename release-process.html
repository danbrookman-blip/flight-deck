The case for change

Why this matters

We are moving to price increases and sales-led comms. Features have to land with impact, and the team has to feel confident selling them. Today they do not always have what they need.

Prep happens too late

Marketing and CS build assets while the feature is still moving, so we arrive under-prepared and the work gets reworked.

No shared view

Teams cannot see what is coming until it is nearly here, so there is no time to plan a proper launch around it.

Every release looks the same

A hotfix and a flagship feature get treated alike. Big things do not get the runway they need; small things get slowed down.

The fix: tier the work, and put everything on one radar. Big features get a full runway. Small fixes still move at pace. Every team sees what is coming the moment development starts.
The process at a glance

How it works, end to end

Five moves take a release from the first line of code to a customer's inbox. Each one has a clear owner and a gate.

1
Dev starts

It goes on the radar with a release horizon.

2
Tier is set

Runway length and prep scope are agreed.

3
Locked

QA passed, UX frozen, Product signs off.

4
Runway

Marketing, CS and Sales prep in parallel.

5
Cleared

Released, customer comms out, logged.

Nothing reaches a customer without being on the radar first. Pace comes from tiering the runway, not from skipping prep.

Tiering the runway

Four tiers, four runways

The tier sets how much runway a release gets and how much prep it needs. It is agreed the moment the tier is set, not negotiated at the end.

TierWhat it isRunwayPrep requiredWho calls it
Major feature New capability, with a pricing or sales angle 2 weeks+ Full: handover deck + video, sales deck, website, social, customer email + video, Intercom article, CS enablement, sales talking points Product proposes, Dan confirms
Minor feature / enhancement A visible improvement to something we already do ~1 week Light: one-pager, Intercom article, lighter customer email, CS note, sales heads-up Product
Improvement / maintenance Under-the-hood work or a small UX change Days Changelog and Intercom entry, CS made aware Product
Bug fix / hotfix A fix, large or small Days Logged to the register; customer note where it touched them Engineering
Everything is logged, down to the smallest fix. That is how customers hear the thing they raised is on its way.
Who owns what

Four lanes, one release

Each lane owns its part of the release and the channels that carry it. Nothing falls between teams.

Product

Owns the feature, the handover deck and video. Sets the tier and horizon, signs off Locked.

ChannelsChangelog, register, handover

Marketing

Owns the external story and assets.

ChannelsCustomer email, website, social, campaign assets

Customer Success

Owns customer readiness and the confidence to support.

ChannelsIntercom articles, help docs, in-app, training

Sales

Owns the commercial story and the pricing context.

ChannelsSales deck, talking points, customer wins

Telling the customer

The customer comms standard

Every major and minor release tells the customer what is coming, how it works, and links a video.

The email

"Hi [name]", what is coming, when, and what is in it for them. One link to the video. Calm Airship product styling.

The video

A short walkthrough: what it does and how to use it. Linked from the email and the article.

The Intercom article

Step by step, how it works. Live before release. The evergreen reference CS points to.

Sign-off

Nothing moves without sign-off

Five gates carry a release from dev start to cleared. Each one has an owner who says go.

1Gate 1 · On the radarProduct logs the item and a horizon at dev start.
2Gate 2 · Tier setProduct proposes the tier; Dan confirms Major.
3Gate 3 · LockedQA passed, UX frozen, Product signs off. The runway starts.
4Gate 4 · ReadyCS, Marketing and Sales each confirm readiness.
5Gate 5 · ClearedGo given. Release, comms out, logged to the register.

The tie-breaker

If a lane says "not ready" at Gate 4, the call to ship or hold sits with Dan. The process surfaces the decision; it does not stall on it.
