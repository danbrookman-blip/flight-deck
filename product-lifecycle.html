One weekly seam‑check, not another L10

The lifecycle is one process that runs sideways across teams who meet in vertical L10s. Bolt it on as another meeting and it gets dropped the first busy week. So we do not. The board carries the status, one thin weekly review keeps the live items honest, and every team works its own slice inside the L10 it already has.

Not bi‑weekly. Not another L10. One weekly seam‑check.

Why not just add a meeting

The two obvious options are both wrong

Adoption is stalling because a cross‑functional workflow has been bolted onto meetings built around single teams. The fix is not more meeting, it is the right thin layer on top of what we already run.

Option one

Bi‑weekly is too slow

Minor releases move in about a week, and the runway prep runs across that week. A fortnightly check means something can go from radar to shipped with no cross‑functional look‑in between. The seam we are trying to watch is exactly where things fall through.

Option two

A second L10 gets dropped

A second ninety‑minute L10 lands on the same Product, CS, Marketing and Commercial people who already sit in departmental L10s. It is the first thing cut when a week gets tight, so the process it was meant to hold quietly stops happening.

It is a horizontal process mismatched onto vertical L10s. Fix the mismatch with a thin seam‑check and the board as the source of truth. Do not add another meeting for people to drop.
The four pillars

What actually makes it stick

Four moves, answering the four questions: how often we meet, who owns it, how Marketing buys in, and how we bake it into the operating rhythm so it survives the handover.

Cadence

Not bi‑weekly, not a second L10. The board is the async source of truth. Add one thin weekly runway review of live items only, and give each team its slice inside the L10 it already has, through the scorecard.

20 to 30 minutes, Product‑chaired, deliberately not an L10.

Ownership

One accountable owner, the Product lead, chairs it and owns four of the five gates. Each lane owns its own gate. Andrew sponsors it as the new operating norm, not the outgoing CEO or CTO. Build it to run on the board and the scorecard.

Runs on the board and the scorecard, not on a person.

Marketing buy‑in

They resist late hand‑offs because they get judged on noise they had no time to plan. Flip it: lead time from the forward view, a vote on the tier at Gate 2, and one measurable they own. Co‑write their runway checklist rather than handing it down.

Lead time, a say on tier, and skin in the game.

Bake it in

Make it a Q3 rock owned by the Product lead: lifecycle embedded and self‑running. Put two or three measurables on the scorecard, link this process from every L10 agenda, and have Andrew sponsor it in his first fortnight so it reads as the norm, not a leaving present.

A rock, a scorecard, and Andrew as sponsor.

The weekly review

Twenty minutes, live items only

Product‑chaired, no slides, no status theatre. The board is already the source of truth, so the review only touches what is moving. Blockers get an owner and a date on the spot, in the spirit of IDS.

On the radar

What is coming

New items that entered development this week, each with a horizon and a tier.

On the runway

What is in prep

Locked items with the lanes prepping in parallel. On track, or not.

Blocked

What is stuck

Anything waiting on a decision or a hand‑off. Named owner, named date, there and then.

Cleared

What shipped

Released this week, comms out, logged to the register. A quick confirm, then done.

Everything else stays in each team’s own L10 scorecard. The seam‑check watches the joins between lanes, not the work inside them.
Who owns it

Accountable to one person, run by the board

PL

Product lead

Accountable owner. Chairs the weekly review, owns four of the five gates, and keeps the board honest. This is the seat the process reports to.

L

Each lane

Product, Marketing, CS and Commercial each own their own gate and their own runway checklist. Accountable for their slice, reviewed in their own L10.

A

Andrew, as sponsor

Sponsors it as the new operating norm in his first fortnight. Not the day‑to‑day referee, the air cover that says this is how we run now.

Built to survive the handover. It leans on the board and the scorecard, not on the outgoing CEO or CTO. If it needed either of them pushing it, it would die at handover, so it does not.
From Monday

How we stand it up

  • ✓Name the Product lead as owner and chair. Andrew sponsors it in his first fortnight, so it lands as the norm and not a leaving present.
  • ✓One 20 to 30 minute weekly runway review goes in the calendar. Live items only. Resist every instinct to turn it into an L10.
  • ✓The board and register are the source of truth. Every live item on it, so most coordination never needs a meeting.
  • ✓Marketing co‑writes its runway checklist and gets the tier vote at Gate 2. Lead time in, last‑minute scramble out.
  • ✓Create the Q3 rock, “lifecycle embedded and self‑running”, with two or three scorecard measurables live: majors with a full runway before Locked, everything logged, comms on time.
  • ✓Link this page from every L10 agenda, so the documented process is one click from where the teams already meet.
