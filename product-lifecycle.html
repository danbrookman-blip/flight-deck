The whole journey

One pipeline, end to end

Every change runs the same path, whatever its size and wherever it came from. The build decides what we ship. The runway decides how the world hears about it. Nothing reaches a customer without going on the radar first, and the biggest bets, the Projects, go on it earliest.

Stage one  /  Where the work comes from

Two streams, one backlog

Work enters from several doors, not just one. It all lands in a single backlog in Linear and is sized by complexity, so nothing gets built on a hunch and nothing gets lost in someone's notes.

Stream one  /  day to day

Requests, raised in Canny

The team and our customers raise day-to-day requests in Canny, and most of these are direct customer asks. Customer Success shepherds them so those requests get real attention rather than being buried, then they are pulled into Linear, sized, and picked up by a Pod to move quickly.

Stream two  /  the bigger bets

Projects, chosen deliberately

Larger initiatives of one to three months. These are the bets we choose to make, not the steady flow of requests, and they come from across the business rather than a single inbox. They join the product roadmap and, once committed, are protected.

Where Projects come from

Customer intimacy interviews Customer demand The imagination of people across the business Competitor analysis The product roadmap, as it evolves
Who decides

Stakeholders agree what we build

The bigger bets are not one person's call. Product, Customer Success, Commercial and Marketing come together to weigh what matters and agree what goes on the roadmap. The CEO holds the final say on what we commit to, but the decision is made in the room, with the people closest to customers in it.

Once it is in flight

The roadmap is protected

When a Project is committed and under way, we do not interrupt it. New ideas are logged and queued for the next decision, they do not barge into work already in flight. That is how Projects actually land, rather than stalling halfway.

Forward view  /  Projects

The big ones, seen coming

A Project is the largest build size, one to three months of work. Because it takes time, the teams that have to get it ready need to see it coming down the track early, so planning can start in good time rather than in a scramble at the end.

At roadmap commitment

It goes on the forward view

The moment a Project is committed to the roadmap, it is visible to every team, with a rough horizon. This is awareness, not a starting gun: teams can see it coming and plan capacity around it. No prep work begins yet.

At build start

Parallel planning begins

When the Project enters build, the lanes start their prep alongside engineering rather than waiting for the handover at the end. The longer the build, the more this early, parallel start earns its place.

On the runway

Tier confirmed on impact

As it nears release, the release tier is confirmed, set by how much the customer needs to hear, not by how long it took to build. From here it runs the same runway as everything else.

A big build is not always a big rollout. Ask Toggle was a major build that reached customers quickly and lightly, without the full two-week runway. So we decide the tier on customer impact, when the Project enters build and again as it nears the runway, and never assume it from how long the build took. Build size sets the heads-up; impact sets the tier.
Stage two  /  Build

The AI builds. We decide.

Every change runs an AI‑led loop with a person steering at the points that matter. The AI plans and executes; humans decide and validate. This is the AI‑DLC development cycle in brief.

1AI
Proposes

Its first‑cut plan for how it will tackle the work.

2You / gate
Review

Check scope and depth, change what is wrong, then let it run.

3AI
Executes

Runs the approved plan, pausing at each meaningful step.

4You / gate
Validate

Confirm the result against the goal it set out to meet.

Three phases

Inception, Construction, Operations

Work out what to build and why, build it in Bolts, then run it and keep it healthy. Each phase hands richer context to the next.

How work is sized

Intent › Unit › Bolt

One vocabulary from the goal down to the slice you build today. A Bolt runs in hours to days; a Project, the largest, is many Units across one to three months.

Who runs it

Pods and Mobs

A Pod is the standing team of four to six that owns an outcome end to end. A Mob is that same team in session with the AI.

Where it starts

Brownfield first

Most work is changes to Airship and Toggle, so the loop reads the existing code before it proposes anything.

The guardrail

Human‑in‑the‑Loop

A person signs off at every gate that matters. The gates only work if you actually use them, so slow down at the checkpoints.

Adaptive depth

Sized to the change

A hotfix skips most stages and goes near‑straight to the fix. A new feature gets the full run. Not scripted, but steered.

That is the AI‑DLC development cycle in brief. The rest of this page covers the handover from the build to the customer.

The handover

The moment dev starts, it goes on the radar

This is where build becomes broadcast. As soon as work begins, it is logged with a tier and a horizon, and every team can see it. The tier reflects how much the customer needs to hear, not how long it took to build, so a long Project can still be a light release. Big features get a full runway; small fixes still move at pace.

TierWhat it isRunwayPrep requiredWho calls it

Minor / enhancement A visible improvement to something we already do ~ 1 week Light: one‑pager, Intercom article, lighter customer email, CS note, sales heads‑up Product
Improvement / maint. Under‑the‑hood work or a small UX change Days Changelog and Intercom entry, CS made aware Product

Everything is logged, down to the smallest fix. That is how a customer hears that the thing they raised is on its way.
Stage three  /  The runway

Four lanes, one release

While the work is being finished, four lanes prep in parallel, so the feature lands with impact and the team is confident to sell it. This is the product marketing engine: the prep happens on a runway, not in a last‑minute scramble.

P

Product

Owns the feature, the handover deck and video. Sets the tier and horizon, signs off Locked, and briefs the lanes on what it does and who it is for.

Changelog · register · handover

CS

Customer Success

Briefed first on what is being built and what it does, then makes customers ready: support articles written and how‑to videos recorded before release.

Intercom · Academy · in‑app · training

M

Marketing

Owns the external story and assets, and arrives with everything needed to push it across our channels: email, website, social and campaign assets.

Email · website · social · campaigns

C

Commercial / Sales

Owns the commercial story: whether it needs pricing, whether it joins the feature lists on the website, and telling package customers (WinFlex and similar) it is being added to their account.

The audiences

Who hears about it, and when

A release is not just shipped, it is told. Five audiences hear about what we are building, each at the right moment and depth. The closer the relationship, the earlier and deeper the conversation.

We do not treat every audience the same. Professionals and beta partners are brought in before release, one to one, to shape the work and carry it first. Existing customers hear it at launch. Prospects and the wider industry hear it through the channels that set the pace for the sector.

It runs across both brands. An Airship release reaches Airship customers and professionals; a Toggle release reaches Toggle's. Same pipeline, the right audience each time.

1

Professionals

Our super users and the network we build individually. Brought in before release, one to one, to pressure‑test the feature and carry it into their venues first. First to know, first to use it.

When: pre‑release, individually
2

Beta users

The larger projects where we want real customer feedback to shape future development. They see features early, feed back through Canny, and stay in the loop on what is coming next as a standing relationship, not a one‑off.

When: pre‑release and ongoing
3

Existing customers

Every customer hears what is coming in advance, then how to use it at release: an email, a short video and a live Intercom article. Nothing lands as a surprise.

When: ahead of, and at, release
4

Prospects

Know what we are building. The roadmap and new capabilities feed the sales story, so prospects buy into where Airship and Toggle are going, not only where they are today.

When: at release, through Sales
5

The wider industry

The bigger moments go out through our channels: website, social, the trade press and events, so the market sees the pace we are setting across hospitality.

When: at and after release, via channels
Telling the customer

The customer comms standard

Every major and minor release tells the customer what is coming, how it works, and links a video. Calm product styling in the right brand, Airship or Toggle.

The email

What is coming, and why it matters

"Hi [name]", what is coming, when, and what is in it for them. One link to the video. Brand‑correct, calm product styling.

The video

A short walkthrough

What it does and how to use it. Recorded by CS during the runway, linked from both the email and the article.

The Intercom article

The evergreen reference

Step by step, how it works. Live before release. The reference CS points to, on the Academy.

Sign‑off

Nothing moves without sign‑off

The build has its own human gates inside the loop. These five sit on top, governing the release itself. Each one is owned by the lane that runs that step, not by one person refereeing the lot.

Gate 1
1
On the radar

Product logs the item and a horizon at dev start.

Gate 2
2
Tier set

Product proposes the tier. For a Project, the stakeholder group agrees it and the CEO has final say on what we commit to.

Gate 3
3
Locked

QA passed, UX frozen, Product signs off. The runway starts.

Gate 4
4
Ready

CS, Marketing and Sales each own and confirm their own readiness.

Gate 5
5
Cleared

Product gives the go. Release, comms out, logged to the register.

Who owns the call. The lanes own their own gates. The CEO holds the final say on what we build and on Major releases, and breaks a genuine deadlock, used rarely, not as a routine referee. The process surfaces the decision; it does not stall on it.
